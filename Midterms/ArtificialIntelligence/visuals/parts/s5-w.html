Question 1 · Open question · Lecture 3.1, Evaluating learning algorithms

Evaluating learning algorithms

  1. (a)Why do we evaluate machine learning models? Give the five reasons from the lecture.
  2. (b)Define overfitting and underfitting in terms of training and test performance.
  3. (c)Give the formulas of MSE, RMSE, MAE and R², and say what each one tells you.

(a) What to write

  • The goal is not only a low training error but good performance on new, unseen data. Evaluation shows the strengths, the weaknesses and what to improve.
  • 1. Generalization ability: will the model work on new data? Evaluation detects over- and underfitting.
  • 2. Model selection: metrics give a standard way to compare candidate models.
  • 3. Parameter tuning: measure the effect of different hyperparameter settings.
  • 4. Understanding strengths and weaknesses: e.g. good in high-dimensional spaces but bad with noisy data.
  • 5. Benchmarking: compare with baselines, previous models and competitors.

(b) What to write

  • Overfitting: the model performs well on training data but poorly on test data. It has learned the noise of the training set.
  • Underfitting: the model performs poorly on both. It is too simple for the pattern in the data.
  • The metrics are computed by feeding a test set to the trained model, so the gap between training and test error is what you look at.

(c) Formulas and meaning

MSE  = (1/m) • Σ (hθ(x)⁽ⁱ⁾ − y⁽ⁱ⁾)²
RMSE = √MSE
MAE  = (1/m) • Σ |hθ(x)⁽ⁱ⁾ − y⁽ⁱ⁾|
R²   = 1 − Σ (y⁽ⁱ⁾ − hθ(x)⁽ⁱ⁾)² / Σ (y⁽ⁱ⁾ − ȳ)²
MetricWhat it tells you
MSEaverage squared error; sensitive to large errors (outliers)
RMSEthe error in the same units as the target
MAEaverage absolute error; less sensitive to large errors than MSE
R²share of the target's variance the model explains; close to 1 = good fit, 0 = no better than predicting the mean
  • MSE here uses 1/m. The cost J(θ) used for gradient descent has 1/2m.
  • Slide 26 example, m = 10: errors h − y = 1, −1, −2, −5, 8, 0, −4, −2, 1, −1.
Σ (h − y)² = 1 + 1 + 4 + 25 + 64 + 0 + 16 + 4 + 1 + 1 = 117
MSE  = 117 / 10 = 11.7
RMSE = √11.7 = 3.42
Σ |h − y| = 1 + 1 + 2 + 5 + 8 + 0 + 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 25
MAE  = 25 / 10 = 2.5
ȳ = 540 / 10 = 54,  Σ (y − ȳ)² = 8440
R²   = 1 − 117 / 8440 = 0.986

Result: MSE 11.7, RMSE 3.42, MAE 2.5, R² 0.986. One error of 8 gives 64 of the 117 in the MSE sum but only 8 of the 25 in the MAE sum.

Exam line

Evaluate for generalization ability, model selection, parameter tuning, strengths and weaknesses, and benchmarking. Overfitting: good on training data, poor on test data. Underfitting: poor on both. MSE is sensitive to large errors, RMSE is in the target's units, MAE is less sensitive to large errors, and R² close to 1 is a good fit.

Question 2 · Open question · Lecture 4.1, Decision tree

Decision trees

  1. (a)Explain the terms root node, decision node, leaf node, splitting, sub-tree, pruning, and parent and child node.
  2. (b)Compare the decision tree algorithms ID3, C4.5 and CART.
  3. (c)Define entropy and information gain. How does the tree pick the attribute for a node? What are the main problems of decision trees, and how are they handled?

(a) What to write

(b) What to write

ID3C4.5CART
Split criterionentropy / information gaingain ratio (textbook context)Gini impurity
Splitsone branch per valueone branch per valuebinary
Datacategorical attributeshandles missing data and continuous attributescategorical and numerical

(c) What to write

  • Entropy E(S) = −p₊ log₂ p₊ − p₋ log₂ p₋ measures impurity: 0 for a pure node, 1 for a 50/50 node. Higher entropy means lower purity.
  • Information gain IG(S, a) = E(S) − Σᵥ (|Sᵥ|/|S|) E(Sᵥ): how much the entropy decreases after splitting on a. Each child is weighted by its share of the rows.
  • Choosing the attribute: at each node compute IG for every attribute and take the highest. A node that is 100% pure becomes a leaf. Then repeat on every branch.
  • Problems: trees overfit easily (they capture noise); the greedy split is only locally optimal; they are sensitive to outliers and to the sample size; missing values must be imputed first.
  • Handling: pruning and stopping criteria (max depth, min samples per node, IG threshold; textbook context for the list), or a random forest of many trees.
Exam line

Root node: the whole dataset starts here; decision node: split further; leaf: final class; pruning removes nodes to prevent overfitting. ID3 uses entropy / information gain, C4.5 handles missing data and continuous attributes, CART uses Gini and does classification and regression. Pick the attribute with the highest IG = E(S) − Σ (|Sᵥ|/|S|) E(Sᵥ).

Question 3 · Open question · Lecture 5.2, Data preparation

Feature scaling, encoding and data leakage

  1. (a)Give the formulas of min-max scaling, standardization and robust scaling, and when to use each.
  2. (b)Compare label encoding, one-hot encoding and target encoding.
  3. (c)What is data leakage, and how is it avoided? How should a heavily imbalanced dataset be handled?

(a) What to write

MethodFormulaOutputUse
Min-Max(x − min)/(max − min)[0, 1]neural networks, KNN, SVM; very sensitive to outliers
Standardization (z-score)(x − μ)/σmean 0, std 1, unboundedlinear and logistic regression, PCA
Robust(x − Q2)/(Q3 − Q1)median-centred, IQR-scaleddata with many outliers
  • Why scale: features on very different ranges (age vs income) are weighted unequally.
  • Start with standardization for linear models. Switch to Robust when a boxplot shows heavy outliers you want to keep. Trees do not need scaling.

(b) What to write

  • Label encoding (0, 1, 2): for ordinal categories (small < medium < large). On nominal data it invents a false order.
  • One-hot encoding (one 0/1 column per category): for nominal categories with a manageable number of values (Tashkent / Samarkand / Bukhara).
  • Target encoding: replace each category by the mean of the target for it (e.g. average salary per city); for high cardinality (hundreds of categories). It uses y, so it must be fit on the training data only.

(c) What to write

  • Data leakage: information from the test set gets into training, e.g. a scaler, encoder, imputer or SMOTE fit on the whole data. The metrics look great and the model fails in production.
  • Avoid it: split first, then fit every preprocessing step on the training set only, and only transform the test set. Use a time-based split without shuffling for time series.
  • Imbalance (e.g. 9,980 normal vs 20 fraud): a model that always says "normal" gets 99.8% accuracy and recall 0.
  • Use precision, recall, F1 or PR-AUC; a stratified split; and SMOTE (synthetic minority points interpolated between a minority point and its nearest minority neighbours) or class weights, on the training data only.
Exam line

Min-Max (x − min)/(max − min) to [0, 1], sensitive to outliers; standardization (x − μ)/σ for linear models and PCA; Robust (x − Q2)/(Q3 − Q1) for data with many outliers. Label encoding for ordinal, one-hot for nominal, target encoding for high cardinality. Split first and fit every scaler, encoder, imputer and SMOTE on the training set only.

Question 4 · Problem · Lecture 4.2, Support vector machine

Support vector machine by hand

Training data: negative class (y = −1): (1, 2), (0, 1), (1, 0); positive class (y = +1): (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 5). A linear SVM gives w = (0.5, 0.5), b = −2.5.

  1. (a)Check that every point satisfies the hard-margin constraint yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1. Which points are the support vectors?
  2. (b)Write the decision boundary and the two margin lines, and compute the margin width.
  3. (c)Classify the new points (2, 2) and (4, 4) and give their distance to the hyperplane.
  4. (d)Two more training points are added: (3, 3) with y = −1 and (2, 3) with y = +1. Keeping the same w and b, compute their slack ζ and the soft-margin cost J = ½‖w‖² + C Σζᵢ for C = 1 and C = 10.

(a) Worked solution

  • f(x) = w·x + b = 0.5x₁ + 0.5x₂ − 2.5. Compute y·f(x) for every point.
Pointyf(x)y·f(x)Status
(1, 2)−1−11on L2, support vector
(0, 1)−1−22beyond the margin
(1, 0)−1−22beyond the margin
(3, 4)+111on L1, support vector
(4, 3)+111on L1, support vector
(5, 5)+12.52.5beyond the margin
  • All values are ≥ 1, so the constraints hold.
  • The support vectors are the points with y·f(x) = 1, the ones closest to the hyperplane.

Result: every yᵢ·f(xᵢ) ≥ 1; support vectors (1, 2), (3, 4), (4, 3).

(b) and (c) Worked solution

boundary  L:  0.5x₁ + 0.5x₂ − 2.5 = 0   →  x₁ + x₂ = 5
L1 (+1):      w·x + b = +1             →  x₁ + x₂ = 7
L2 (−1):      w·x + b = −1             →  x₁ + x₂ = 3
‖w‖ = √(0.25 + 0.25) = 0.7071          d = 2/‖w‖ = 2.828
check: |(3, 4) − (1, 2)| = √(4 + 4) = 2.828, along w

distance = |f(x)| / ‖w‖
(2, 2):  f = 1 + 1 − 2.5 = −0.5 < 0  →  y = −1,  0.5 / 0.7071 = 0.707  (inside the margin)
(4, 4):  f = 2 + 2 − 2.5 =  1.5 ≥ 0  →  y = +1,  1.5 / 0.7071 = 2.121

Result: boundary x₁ + x₂ = 5, L1: x₁ + x₂ = 7, L2: x₁ + x₂ = 3, d = 2.828; (2, 2) → −1 at 0.707, (4, 4) → +1 at 2.121.

(d) Worked solution

ζ = max(0, 1 − y·f(x))
(3, 3), y = −1:  f = 0.5,  y·f = −0.5,  ζ = 1.5   (> 1: misclassified, on the positive side)
(2, 3), y = +1:  f = 0,    y·f = 0,     ζ = 1     (on the hyperplane, inside the margin)
½‖w‖² = ½ · 0.5 = 0.25        Σζ = 1.5 + 1 = 2.5
C = 1:   J = 0.25 + 1 · 2.5  = 2.75
C = 10:  J = 0.25 + 10 · 2.5 = 25.25

Result: ζ = 1.5 and 1; J = 2.75 for C = 1 and 25.25 for C = 10. With a large C the violations dominate the cost, so training would push w and b to fix them, narrowing the margin.

Exam line

Hard margin: yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1, and the support vectors are the points with equality. Margin lines w·x + b = ±1, width d = 2/‖w‖, distance to the hyperplane |w·x + b|/‖w‖. Soft margin: ζ = max(0, 1 − y·f(x)) and J = ½‖w‖² + C Σζ; a large C penalizes violations strongly and gives a smaller margin.

Question 6 · Problem · Lecture 4.1, Decision tree and random forest

Decision tree and random forest

Will a customer buy a computer?

  1. (a)Compute the entropy of the dataset.
  2. (b)Compute the information gain of Age, Student and Credit, and choose the root.
  3. (c)Build the complete tree with ID3.
  4. (d)Classify x = {Age = Youth, Student = No, Credit = Fair} with your tree.
  5. (e)A random forest uses your tree as T1 and two more trees built on bootstrapped data:
    T2: Student? Yes → Yes; No → Credit? (Fair → Yes, Excellent → No)
    T3: Credit? Fair → Yes; Excellent → Age? (Youth → Yes, Middle → Yes, Senior → No)
    What does the forest predict for x?

(a) Worked solution

9 Yes, 5 No
E(S) = −(9/14) log₂(9/14) − (5/14) log₂(5/14) = 0.410 + 0.530 = 0.940

(b) Worked solution

AttributeValue: Yes/No (entropy)Weighted EIG
AgeYouth 2/3 (0.971), Middle 4/0 (0), Senior 3/2 (0.971)10/14 · 0.971 = 0.6940.247
StudentNo 4/4 (1), Yes 5/1 (0.650)8/14 · 1 + 6/14 · 0.650 = 0.8500.090
CreditFair 6/2 (0.811), Excellent 3/3 (1)8/14 · 0.811 + 6/14 · 1 = 0.8920.048

Result: Age is the root (IG 0.247). Age = Middle is pure (4/0), so it is a Yes leaf.

(c) Worked solution

  • Youth (rows 1, 2, 8, 9, 11: 2 Yes / 3 No, E = 0.971): Student gives No 0/3 and Yes 2/0, IG = 0.971; Credit gives Fair 1/2 and Excellent 1/1, IG = 0.020. Split on Student.
  • Senior (rows 4, 5, 6, 10, 14: 3 Yes / 2 No, E = 0.971): Credit gives Fair 3/0 and Excellent 0/2, IG = 0.971; Student gives No 2/1 and Yes 1/1, IG = 0.020. Split on Credit.
  • All leaves are now pure, so ID3 stops.

(d) and (e) Worked solution

  • (d) x = {Youth, No, Fair}: Age = Youth, then Student = No, so T1 says No.
  • (e) T1: No (Youth, Student = No). T2: Student = No, then Credit = Fair, so Yes. T3: Credit = Fair, so Yes.
  • Majority vote 2 to 1: the forest predicts Yes.
  • The forest can disagree with any single tree. The vote of several different trees is what reduces overfitting.

Result: T1 says No, the forest says Yes (2 votes to 1).

Exam line

E(S) = 0.940 for 9 Yes / 5 No. IG(S, a) = E(S) − Σ (|Sᵥ|/|S|) E(Sᵥ); the highest IG becomes the node, and a pure node becomes a leaf. A random forest combines many trees built on bootstrapped data by majority vote.
